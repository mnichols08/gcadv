Most people come to Swallow Falls State Park for Muddy Creek Falls.

That makes sense. The waterfall is the obvious reason to go.

But Swallow Falls is more interesting when you slow down. The gorge, smaller falls, old-growth trees, natural trail, and local history all matter. If you only walk in, photograph Maryland's tallest waterfall, and leave, you have technically seen the headline while missing a lot of the park.

The best version of a visit is simple: see the waterfall, walk the Canyon Trail carefully, look up at the forest, and give the place a little more time than a quick photo stop.

## Start with Muddy Creek Falls

{% photo "Muddy Creek Falls", "Photo placeholder for the main waterfall view.", "wide", "swallow" %}

Muddy Creek Falls drops 53 feet and is identified by Maryland DNR as Maryland's tallest free-falling waterfall.

It is large enough to feel like a real destination, but it does not require a long hike to reach. That combination is a big part of why Swallow Falls is so popular with Deep Creek and Garrett County visitors.

The falls are especially good for first-time visitors because they give you a dramatic piece of western Maryland scenery without needing to turn the day into a major expedition.

Still, the waterfall is not the whole park. Treat it as the starting point, not the finish line.

## The Canyon Trail

The Canyon Trail is approximately 1.25 miles and passes through the Youghiogheny River gorge.

Along the way, visitors encounter Muddy Creek Falls, the Swallow Falls area, and the park's smaller falls, including Upper Swallow Falls and Lower Swallow Falls.

The distance is short. The surface is not a paved town walk.

Short does not mean flat. The Canyon Trail is only about 1.25 miles, but you are still walking over roots, rock, stairs, and uneven natural surfaces. Families regularly describe it as manageable with active kids, but that is community experience, not an official accessibility rating.

Wet natural surfaces matter here. Rain, spray, mud, snow, ice, roots, rock, and stairs can change how the trail feels. Take your time and choose footwear for a natural trail, not for a sidewalk.

## Look up at the forest

{% photo "Old-growth hemlock forest", "Photo placeholder for the eastern hemlock and white pine grove.", "offset", "swallow" %}

The old-growth forest is one of the most distinctive parts of Swallow Falls.

Maryland DNR identifies the Youghiogheny Grove Natural Area as containing old-growth eastern hemlock and white pine. Some trees are documented as being at least roughly 360 years old.

That deserves real attention.

The waterfall is louder, easier to photograph, and more obvious. The old trees are quieter, but they are part of what makes this park feel different from a standard waterfall stop.

Some of the trees around you were already old before the United States existed. That fact changes the walk if you let it.

## Why is it called Swallow Falls?

The park's name is tied to the gorge itself.

Historical explanations connect "Swallow Falls" to rock formations and the cliff swallows that once nested in the area. It is a small detail, but a useful one: the name is not just a pretty label for the waterfall.

It comes from the landscape people were noticing here before the modern state-park visit became the default way most of us experience it.

## Ford, Edison and Firestone camped here

{% photo "Garrett County, July 1921", "Archival photo placeholder for Henry Ford, Thomas Edison, Harvey Firestone, families and companions during the Vagabonds era.", "", "swallow" %}

In July 1921, Henry Ford, Thomas Edison, Harvey Firestone, their families, and companions camped at what is now Swallow Falls State Park as part of the automobile-era camping group known as the Vagabonds.

John Burroughs is associated with the Vagabonds historically, but he died earlier in 1921 and was not part of this Swallow Falls camp.

The Garrett County visit drew attention locally. Historical accounts describe crowds gathering in Oakland as people hoped to see Ford, Edison, and Firestone pass through town before the group continued toward the camp near Muddy Creek.

Maryland's first state forester, Fred W. Besley, is part of the story as well. The visit helped draw attention to forest conservation and outdoor recreation in Maryland.

There is also a local-history feature tied to Edison: Maryland DNR documents a Thomas Edison Sitting or Thinking Stone near Muddy Creek.

The celebrity story is not the whole reason to visit Swallow Falls. It is simply one more layer in a park that already has waterfalls, old-growth forest, and a gorge worth noticing.

## If you are visiting with kids

Swallow Falls can be a very good family outing.

The trail is compact, the scenery changes often, and children have obvious things to look for: waterfalls, huge trees, river views, rocks, bridges, and the gorge.

The important caveat is the same one adults need: short trail does not equal flat trail.

Parents and community discussions frequently recommend Swallow Falls for families, especially active children who are comfortable on natural surfaces. That does not make it easy for every child, every stroller, or every mobility situation.

Use extra caution after rain, near wet rock, on stairs, and during winter conditions.

## Pair it with Herrington Manor

Swallow Falls pairs naturally with Herrington Manor for visitors who want another nearby stop.

Many visitors use Swallow Falls as the waterfall-and-gorge part of the day, then continue to Herrington Manor for a quieter lake and forest setting. That pairing comes up often in visitor discussions because it gives you two different moods without turning the day into a long regional road trip.

There is also a roughly 5.5-mile trail connection between Swallow Falls and Herrington Manor through Garrett State Forest. That is a different kind of outing than the short Canyon Trail and should be planned as a longer trail day, not as an accidental add-on.

The park also has a campground with wooded campsites and camper cabins, so Swallow Falls does not have to be only a drive-in, walk, leave experience.

## Know before you go

Park hours are currently listed as 8 a.m. to sunset.

Day-use reservations are currently required on weekends and holidays from Memorial Day weekend through Labor Day. Maryland's current system uses timed-entry / reservation information, so check the current Maryland DNR page and reservation system before driving over during the busy season.

Pets are not permitted in the Swallow Falls day-use area, including the Canyon Trail, from May 23 through September 7, 2026. Service animals are treated separately under park rules.

Day-use fees can change, so this guide does not try to preserve a fee table. Check the current DNR information before visiting.

Wet natural surfaces can be slippery. Snow and ice can make the trail hazardous. Winter or wet-weather visits can be beautiful, but conditions matter more than photographs.

Do not use older visitor stories as permission to swim, slide, jump, or access unofficial water areas. Current park rules and safety conditions come first.

## Is it worth the trip?

Yes, especially for first-time Deep Creek visitors, waterfall and nature visitors, families comfortable on natural trails, people interested in local history, and anyone who wants a relatively compact outdoor outing.

Swallow Falls is famous for a good reason.

But the best reason to go is not only the waterfall. It is the combination of Muddy Creek Falls, the Canyon Trail, the gorge, old-growth hemlock and white pine, smaller falls, and the odd fact that Ford, Edison, and Firestone once camped here.

You do not need to spend the whole day here.

You should give it enough time to be more than a photograph.
